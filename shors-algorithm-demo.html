Why a quantum computer can break RSA

RSA encryption relies on one thing being hard for a classical computer: factoring a very large number N into its two secret primes. Shor's algorithm gives a quantum computer a way to find those primes efficiently — not by guessing, but by finding a hidden repeating pattern in a sequence of numbers.

2¹, 2², 2³, 2⁴ … mod 15 — watch it repeat every 4 steps. That repeat length is the "period," and it's the key to everything below.

02 · The core idea

Find the period, and the factors fall out

Shor's algorithm doesn't factor N directly. Instead, it picks a random number a and looks at the sequence a¹, a², a³, …, each one reduced mod N. Because there are only a limited number of possible remainders, this sequence always eventually repeats. The length of that repeat is called the period, written r. A quantum computer finds r almost instantly using superposition — a step that would take a classical computer a very long time for large N.

Periodicity — how often a repeating sequence cycles back to its starting value. Here, it's how many multiplications it takes before aˣ mod N returns to 1.
"mod N" means "the remainder after dividing by N." 17 mod 15 = 2, because 17 divided by 15 leaves a remainder of 2.
Greatest Common Divisor (GCD) — the largest number that divides evenly into two other numbers. gcd(12, 18) = 6. The Euclidean algorithm finds it quickly by repeated division.

03 · From period to factors

Turning r into the actual primes

Once the period r is known, ordinary classical math finishes the job. If r is even, compute ar/2 mod N, then take the GCD of that value ±1 with N. Those two GCDs are usually the hidden prime factors.

04 · Why this actually matters

From N = 15 to a real RSA key

Factoring 15 is something you could do in your head. RSA in the real world uses numbers that are hundreds of digits long — and that gap in scale is the entire reason quantum computing is a cybersecurity concern rather than a party trick.

This demo — N = 15trivial, even by hand
Real-world RSA-2048far beyond today's hardware

A widely cited 2019 estimate (Gidney & Ekerå) put the requirement to break a 2048-bit RSA key at roughly 20 million error-corrected qubits running for about 8 hours. Today's largest quantum processors have on the order of a few thousand physical qubits, and none yet have the error correction Shor's algorithm needs at that scale — which is why RSA is still considered safe for now, but not indefinitely.

05 · The defense

Fighting quantum with quantum-resistant math

If a large enough quantum computer eventually makes Shor's algorithm practical, the fix isn't to abandon math-based security — it's to stop relying on problems that quantum computers happen to be good at. That's the idea behind post-quantum cryptography (PQC): new algorithms built on math problems that stay hard even for a quantum computer.

Post-quantum cryptography — cryptography designed to run on today's ordinary (classical) computers, but built on problems believed to resist attack from quantum computers too, unlike factoring or discrete log.

In 2024, NIST finalized its first PQC standards, expected to gradually replace RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography across the internet over the next decade.

A different approach — QKD: instead of a new hard math problem, Quantum Key Distribution (QKD) protocols like BB84 use quantum mechanics itself for security — measuring a quantum-encoded key reveals any eavesdropper by disturbing the system.
