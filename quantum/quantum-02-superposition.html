Same odds, different states

A qubit in superposition isn't secretly 0 or secretly 1. Its amplitudes set the odds of each answer, and they can also be negative. Two qubits can have identical odds and still behave completely differently, and that difference is where quantum computing gets its power.

02 · Measurement

Looking forces a choice, and the choice sticks

Measuring a qubit in superposition gives a random result weighted by the amplitudes. Afterward the qubit is no longer in superposition: it has collapsed to whatever you saw. Measure it again and you get the same answer every time.

Collapse — after a measurement, the state becomes the basis state that was observed. The original amplitudes are gone and cannot be recovered from that qubit.

03 · The core idea

Interference: amplitudes can cancel

The Hadamard gate (H) turns |0⟩ into |+⟩, an even 50/50 blend. Apply H a second time and you might expect another coin flip. Instead you get |0⟩ every single time. The two paths leading to |1⟩ have opposite signs and cancel, while the paths to |0⟩ add up. That is interference, and every useful quantum algorithm is built on it.

Hadamard gate — sends |0⟩ to |+⟩ and |1⟩ to |−⟩. As a matrix it is (1/√2)·[[1, 1], [1, −1]]. Page 04 covers it with the other gates.

04 · Choosing what to measure

Measurement bases

So far every measurement has asked "0 or 1?" That's the Z basis (also called the computational or rectilinear basis). You can also ask "plus or minus?" by applying H and then measuring. That's the X basis (the diagonal basis). A state that is certain in one basis is a pure coin flip in the other.

05 · Security connection

A message that changes when someone reads it

Measurement disturbs the state, and the no-cloning theorem says nobody can copy an unknown quantum state to measure the copy instead. Put those together and you get a channel where an eavesdropper can't look without leaving fingerprints.

No-cloning theorem — there is no physical process that takes an unknown qubit and produces two identical copies of it. An attacker can't photocopy a quantum message and read the copy at leisure.
Where this goes: BB84 quantum key distribution (page 07) encodes key bits in randomly chosen Z or X bases. An eavesdropper who guesses the wrong basis collapses the photon and introduces errors the legitimate users can detect.
