Why a quantum computer can break RSA

RSA encryption relies on one thing being hard for a classical computer: factoring a very large number N into its two secret primes. Shor's algorithm gives a quantum computer a way to find those primes efficiently, not by guessing, but by finding a hidden repeating pattern in a sequence of numbers.

02 · The core idea

Find the period, and the factors fall out

Shor's algorithm doesn't factor N directly. Instead, it picks a random number a and looks at the sequence a¹, a², a³, …, each one reduced mod N. Because there are only a limited number of possible remainders, this sequence always eventually repeats. The length of that repeat is called the period, written r. A quantum computer finds r efficiently using superposition and interference, a step that would take a classical computer an impractically long time for large N.

Periodicity — how often a repeating sequence cycles back to its starting value. Here, it's how many multiplications it takes before aˣ mod N returns to 1.
"mod N" means "the remainder after dividing by N." 17 mod 15 = 2, because 17 divided by 15 leaves a remainder of 2.
Greatest Common Divisor (GCD) — the largest number that divides evenly into two other numbers. gcd(12, 18) = 6. The Euclidean algorithm finds it quickly by repeated division.

03 · Inside the quantum step

What the quantum computer actually measures

The simulator above lists the sequence one term at a time, which is the slow classical way. The quantum computer instead puts an input register into superposition over every x at once, computes aˣ mod N into a second register, and then applies the quantum Fourier transform (QFT). Interference makes the measurement land on a multiple of Q/r, where Q is the size of the input register.

Quantum Fourier transform — the quantum version of the discrete Fourier transform, which picks out repeating frequencies in a signal. Applied to a periodic register, it concentrates the amplitude on outcomes spaced Q/r apart.

04 · From period to factors

Turning r into the actual primes

Once the period r is known, ordinary classical math finishes the job. If r is even, compute ar/2 mod N, then take the GCD of that value ±1 with N. Those two GCDs are usually the hidden prime factors.

05 · Why this actually matters

From N = 15 to a real RSA key

Factoring 15 is something you could do in your head. RSA in the real world uses numbers that are hundreds of digits long, and that gap in scale is the entire reason quantum computing is a cybersecurity concern rather than a party trick.

Today's largest processors have on the order of a thousand physical qubits, and none yet run the error correction Shor's algorithm needs at scale. RSA is still safe for now, but the target keeps getting closer.

06 · The defense

Fighting quantum with quantum-resistant math

If a large enough quantum computer eventually makes Shor's algorithm practical, the fix isn't to abandon math-based security. It's to stop relying on problems that quantum computers happen to be good at. That's the idea behind post-quantum cryptography (PQC).

Post-quantum cryptography — cryptography designed to run on today's ordinary (classical) computers, but built on problems believed to resist attack from quantum computers too, unlike factoring or discrete log.

In 2024, NIST finalized its first PQC standards, which are expected to gradually replace RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography across the internet. Page 09 goes deeper into how they work and how organizations migrate.

A different approach, QKD: instead of a new hard math problem, quantum key distribution protocols like BB84 use quantum mechanics itself for security. Measuring a quantum-encoded key reveals any eavesdropper by disturbing the system. Page 07 has the hands-on simulation.
