A qubit is not just a fancier bit

Every computer you've ever used stores information as bits: each one is a 0 or a 1, nothing in between. A quantum computer uses qubits, which can sit in a weighted blend of 0 and 1 until the moment you look. That one change is why the number of qubits matters so much more than the number of bits.

Qubit (quantum bit) — the basic unit of quantum information. Physically it can be an ion, a photon, or a tiny superconducting circuit. Mathematically it's a pair of numbers (amplitudes) that describe how likely it is to read 0 or 1.

02 · The core idea

Writing a qubit down: ket notation

Physicists write quantum states inside a ket, the | ⟩ symbol. The two basic states are |0⟩ and |1⟩, the quantum versions of a classical 0 and 1. Each is really a two-row column of numbers, called a vector.

Ket — the |name⟩ symbol from Dirac (bra-ket) notation. It just labels a state. |0⟩ is read "ket zero." Underneath, every ket is a column vector.
|0⟩=10 |1⟩=01
|ψ⟩=α|0⟩ + β|1⟩=αβ
rule: |α|² + |β|² = 1  ·  P(read 0) = |α|²  ·  P(read 1) = |β|²

The numbers α and β are called amplitudes. Squaring an amplitude gives a probability, which is why they must square-and-add to exactly 1. A qubit with both amplitudes nonzero is in superposition (the next page covers it in depth).

Amplitude — the number in front of each ket. Amplitudes can be negative or even complex, which is what lets quantum algorithms make wrong answers cancel out. On this page we keep them positive to focus on probability.

Common misconception: a qubit does not "store 0 and 1 at the same time" in a way you can read out. Every measurement gives you one ordinary bit. The amplitudes only show up as statistics across many runs, which is exactly what the tally above demonstrates.

03 · More than one qubit

Two qubits need four numbers

Combine qubits with the tensor product (written ⊗) and the vector doubles in length each time. Two qubits have four basis states, |00⟩ |01⟩ |10⟩ |11⟩, and a two-qubit state carries an amplitude for each one.

Tensor product (⊗) — the way quantum states combine. Multiply each entry of the first vector by the whole second vector and stack the results. A 2-row vector ⊗ a 2-row vector gives a 4-row vector.
|0⟩⊗|1⟩= 10⊗01= 1·01·10·00·1= 0100=|01⟩

04 · Scaling

Add one qubit, double the work

Describing n classical bits takes n numbers. Describing n qubits takes 2ⁿ amplitudes. To simulate a quantum computer on a normal one, you must store every amplitude, roughly 16 bytes each. That doubling is why classical computers can't keep up past about 50 qubits.

05 · Reality check

Why more qubits doesn't mean infinite power

The 2ⁿ amplitudes are real, but you can't simply read them all out. Measuring n qubits gives back exactly n ordinary bits. Quantum algorithms win by arranging amplitudes so that wrong answers cancel and the right answer is likely to be measured. That trick only works for certain problems, and one of those problems happens to be the math behind RSA.

WHAT QUBITS GIVE YOU
A state space that doubles with every qubit, which classical computers can't track at scale.
WHAT THEY DON'T
Free access to all 2ⁿ answers. You still read out only n bits per run.
Security connection: breaking RSA-2048 with Shor's algorithm needs a large number of error-corrected qubits. Recent estimates put that around a million noisy physical qubits, far beyond today's machines. The Shor's Algorithm page in this series walks through why.
